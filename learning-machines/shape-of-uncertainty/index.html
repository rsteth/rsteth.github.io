The shape of uncertainty

What happens when a machine discovers a better way to describe what it already knows?

01 / a bit of uncertainty

What does a better guess tell us?

Suppose I flip a fair coin. Before you see it, there are two equally likely outcomes, and learning which one happened gives you one bit. Flip a second independent coin and you need another bit. The first result tells you nothing about the second.

Letters behave differently. If I give you the first few characters of a sentence, you can often do much better than guessing uniformly. In his experiments on printed English, Claude Shannon estimated bounds of roughly 0.6 to 1.3 bits per character under his historical guessing setup. The interesting part isn’t the exact number. It’s how much uncertainty disappears once you take the relationships between the characters into account.

One way to score a model is to ask how much probability it gives to the answer that actually occurs. A probability of one in eight costs three bits; one in two costs one bit. Average that cost over the same set of predictions, then raise two to that average, and you get perplexity. It behaves like an effective number of choices; it doesn’t tell us which choices the model considered or how it found the answer. Comparisons also need the same test and the same unit. Bits per token and bits per character don’t mean the same thing.

What I’m curious about is what happens inside the machine as that score falls. Has it remembered more examples, or has it found a description that lets it derive many of the examples from one relationship?

02 / discovering the circle

A clock can be a table or a place to move.

Imagine a machine watching the numbers on a clock. It might learn that 2 follows 1, 3 follows 2, and 1 follows 12. Or it could describe all twelve positions with one circle and a rotation. Once it has that description, stepping backward, combining two movements, and going all the way around become things it can work out without seeing each example separately.

Goodfire’s geometric calculator offers a concrete example from Llama 3.1 8B. The researchers describe numbers as occupying phases on several circles with different periods. They found a reusable circuit that acts on those representations, including in arithmetic and calendar tasks. They also intervened on activations and observed changes in outputs. The idealized math is simple: if a number is a phase, adding two numbers amounts to composing rotations. That identity explains the idea, not every operation the actual network performs.

It reminds me of the robot in the previous article. Fourteen joint angles can describe both arms, but a particular useful movement can be much simpler in different coordinates. A representation can change what’s easy to do without changing the task.

03 / different machines, the same object?

Can one model use another model’s map?

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis suggests that different models may come to describe some of the same structure in the world. Picture three people drawing the same coastline, with different scales, orientations and levels of detail. If they captured enough of the same relationships, you might be able to use one person’s map to navigate another’s.

In Plato’s Key, Stanford’s Hazy Research group tested a limited version of that idea on factual recall. They fit a change of coordinates using centering, an orthogonal map and a single scale. Then they replaced one model’s internal state with the transformed state of another while leaving both models’ weights alone. Across 20 pairs drawn from 12 models, the median cross-model swap success was 41%, versus 55% for swaps within the same model. Via an intermediate model, transfer retained a median 99% of direct transfer’s accuracy. That last number is relative, not a 99% factual recall score.

What one study asks is whether a model can compute in a learned geometry. What the other asks is whether two models can use some of the same coordinates. Neither establishes that they learned the same operation. I’d like to test whether computing first and then translating gives the same result as translating first and then computing.

04 / approaching the primitive

Three approximations, and a surprise.

Imagine that the underlying knowledge primitive is a manifold, 𝓜, and that three models have learned different approximations of it. They all get glimpses of the same thing. One keeps fragments, another joins them into a warped surface, and a third recovers something closer to the original.

My starting hunch was that the closer fit would make it easier to move between points the model had never seen and to predict where a move would end. We can put that hunch somewhere it can fail: a synthetic world with simpler geometry and rules we know in advance.

Our first pilot gave three models incomplete movement examples on a 32-stop dial with shuffled labels. A lookup table memorized examples. An unconstrained neural network learned free two-dimensional embeddings. A rotation-based network was given circular topology and a rule for composing moves, but had to learn where the states went and how far a unit move turned.

The rotation prior helped, as we might expect. The surprise was that three of its five runs predicted every tested destination correctly, including for new move lengths, even though their internal distances bore little resemblance to the distances around the original dial. The other two runs learned even windings that collapsed pairs of states.

zₖ(x) = exp(2πikx/N);   zₖ(x + a) = zₖ(x) · zₖ(a)

Multiply the state number by k before placing it on the dial. If k and the number of states share no common factor, every state still has a unique place and the rotation rule still works. The learned space can look wrong by a distance measure and still support the right computation. Perhaps the primitive is the set of relationships that survive these changes of coordinates, rather than a unique surface we should expect every model to recover.

05 / what the experiment can tell us

What has to come into focus?

There are three things worth measuring separately: the fidelity of the representation, the fidelity of the operations we can carry out on it, and the predictive loss. In the pilot we know the real probability distribution. Average predictive loss is the uncertainty built into that distribution plus the penalty for getting the distribution wrong. Lower loss tells us the predictions got better. It doesn’t tell us which representation did the work, or whether the original distances mattered at all.

The first models had different capacities and priors. We gave the winning architecture a circle, and its probability decoder didn’t exactly match our noise model. So this is an exploratory result, not evidence that real language models discover the same surface or that there’s a perplexity threshold for a capability jump. A better next experiment would hold the world fixed across initializations, use unseen compositions and alignment anchors, and compare observation channels that do or don’t reveal the original metric. Then we can look at which changes happen together as each model learns.

We started by asking how close a machine could get to a surface. I think the better question is: what properties does a machine have to preserve in order to know its way around?
